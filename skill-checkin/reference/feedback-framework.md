# Giving Feedback at a Check-In

Feedback is the point of a check-in even when nothing about the plan changes. An athlete who hears nothing but "looks good, keep going" every time learns to stop paying attention to check-ins. Be specific, be honest, and connect what happened back to why it matters for the race.

## Structure

Cover these in order, in conversation — not as a rigid five-bullet report unless the athlete seems to want the compressed version.

### 1. Consistency

- Completion rate for the review window: sessions done vs. scheduled, both running and strength (call out strength separately — it's the one that quietly slips first)
- Pattern, not just percentage: one rough week inside an otherwise solid block reads very differently from a steady one-in-three miss rate. Name the pattern if there is one.
- If strength consistency is dropping while running holds — flag it explicitly. This is the single most common quiet failure mode in these plans (see `coach`'s own principle: "Don't let strength training slip in build/peak phases").

### 2. Load Trend

- Is training load (COROS `queryTrainingLoadAssessment`, or your own read of volume/intensity from what the athlete reported) building, holding, or dropping?
- Does that match what the current phase calls for? Building load in Base is expected; building load in a taper week is a problem worth naming.
- If COROS data is available, mention the load ratio and recovery trend in plain terms ("your body's absorbing the load fine" / "load's climbing faster than recovery is keeping up") rather than reciting raw numbers — see `coach`'s `skill/reference/load-management.md` for what the ratios mean if you need the underlying thresholds.

### 3. What's Going Well

Be concrete. "Great job!" teaches nothing. Instead:

- "Your easy runs have stayed genuinely easy — that's exactly the aerobic base this phase is for."
- "Three-for-three on strength this block. That consistency is exactly what prevents the injuries that usually show up in weeks like these."
- "Your long run pace held steady even as the distance went up — that's a good sign for race-day durability."

### 4. What's a Concern

Also be concrete, and not softened into meaninglessness:

- "Two of your last three quality sessions got cut short. That's worth understanding before we just push through week 8."
- "HR on your easy runs has been creeping up for the same pace over the last two weeks — combined with the sleep data, that reads like accumulating fatigue, not fitness."
- "Strength has dropped to one session in the last three weeks. At this point in the plan that's a real gap, not a rounding error."

Don't manufacture a concern if there isn't one — "nothing concerning this block" is a fine thing to say when it's true.

### 5. Where This Leaves Them

Close the feedback by translating it into race terms:

- On track for the goal as planned
- On track, but the goal itself is worth revisiting (ahead of schedule, or the fitness data suggests a faster time is realistic — or the opposite)
- Off track in a way the plan can absorb (this is where Step 5 of the main workflow — adaptation — comes in)
- Off track in a way that needs a bigger conversation (injury that needs medical input, life circumstances that call for a different race entirely) — say so plainly rather than patching around it

## Tone

Talk like a coach who knows this athlete, not like a report generator. Reference specifics from their plan and history (their stated goals, their limiters from the original assessment, prior check-ins) rather than giving feedback that could apply to anyone.
